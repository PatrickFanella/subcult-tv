# Argument machines: markets, courts, and the usefulness of structured disagreement

Before an autonomous system spends money or changes a shared resource, somebody should be able to stop it. That sounds obvious. In software, the objection often arrives after execution, as a log entry.

The omission is easy to miss at full speed. A signal comes in, a model interprets it, an action goes out. The clean path looks efficient because context and doubt have nowhere to enter.

Markets and courts have procedures for this. A market lets people act on incompatible beliefs through bids and offers. A court assigns roles, admits evidence, hears objections, and records a decision. Both give disagreement authority and consequence, which is why we call them argument machines.

Several SUBCULT projects test the idea in different settings. augr runs market analysis through debate before execution. JuryRigged gives a courtroom simulation fixed phases and jury voting. Subcorp is still incubating. Its design explores roundtable conversations and autonomous workflows in a self-hosted multi-agent system. Read it as work in progress, not as proof that every governance problem has been solved.

Adding another agent does little on its own. An objection matters only when the system knows who can raise it, what evidence supports it, and whether it can pause the next action.

> Autonomy without disagreement is just automation with better branding.

How much review an action needs depends on the stakes. A trading workflow may need exposure limits before an order. A courtroom simulation needs a coherent route from testimony to verdict. An autonomous office needs a way to catch mission drift. Some actions need a risk check, others a dissenting recommendation or a human approval. Afterward the system needs enough history to compare the prediction with the result.

Those pauses are part of the work. They keep a confident output from passing as a settled decision, and they leave a record when the minority view turns out to be right. This is governance done inside product design. The more power a system has to act, the clearer the route to object has to be.

We are not asking for gavels or a trading floor in the software. We are asking for a decision procedure that takes incompatible interpretations seriously before an irreversible step. Execution can stay fast where the stakes are low. Where they rise, the argument belongs in the path, with enough memory to show whose judgment held up.
