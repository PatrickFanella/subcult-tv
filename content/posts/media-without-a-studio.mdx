# Media without a studio: small-crew production in the age of assembly lines

A phone fills up with footage. One clip has the clean audio. Another has the right gesture. The notes are somewhere else, and the edit stalls before anyone sees a rough cut.

This is ordinary production trouble. Every short video passes through decisions about structure, shots, captions, credits, export, and release.

A studio assigns those decisions to departments. A person working alone has the same jobs with less time.

Independent media needs production support that does not seize the work. The stages should be visible, the next decision should be clear, and each handoff should preserve authorship.

Two incubating Subcult projects approach that need at different scales. Cutroom is a proposed collaborative AI video pipeline built around specialized agents, structured handoffs, and attribution.

One Block Cinema is a smartphone filmmaking coach for structured one-to-three-minute shorts. Its constraint starts with the device someone already has and a form small enough to finish.

Neither project is a deployed answer yet. They are attempts to make the production process easier to inhabit without copying a studio hierarchy.

Media automation often focuses on replacement. It can draft a script, synthesize a voice, or assemble a cut. More output does not settle the artistic questions: why this piece, whose view, and who gets credit?

Useful automation carries context between stages. It can organize assets, flag a continuity problem, or prepare captions for review. The author still decides what belongs in the piece and when the piece is done.

That boundary must stay legible. A generated suggestion needs a clear source. Credits need to survive the export. Review needs to happen before distribution, while a person can still change the work.

Small constraints help too. A block gives the shoot a location. A short runtime limits the footage. Available light removes a setup. Each limit cuts a branch from the decision tree and moves the film closer to a finished cut.

The dull parts still matter. Stable filenames keep footage attached to notes. A visible production stage keeps a draft from vanishing between edit and captioning. A plain archive makes later reuse possible.

Platforms offer to absorb much of this work. In return, they shape the format, tune distribution for engagement, and make the archive depend on an account. Convenience can quietly become editorial control.

A workshop model keeps the files and decisions with the people making the piece. Tools can assist without becoming the publisher, the rights holder, or the audience gatekeeper.

> Small media does not need to become content. It needs a workshop.

That workshop may be one person, a phone, and a few dependable steps. It may include software that checks the tedious parts. Its purpose is modest and demanding: help a small crew finish the work without giving away its shape.

The machine can carry the clipboard. The author keeps the final cut.
