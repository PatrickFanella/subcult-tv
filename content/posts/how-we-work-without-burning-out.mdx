# How we work without burning out

We have watched promising collaborative projects thin out through exhaustion. The roadmap still looked fine. The people doing the work had run out of room for it.

This happens even when the idea is sound and the team is capable. Participation starts asking for more attention and emotional energy than people can keep giving. Tech culture has a script for this: ship fast, keep iterating, grind through it. The script turns care into a test of endurance, and neither a community nor the people maintaining it can live on adrenaline.

## What it looks like

Burnout often arrives quietly. Replies take longer. A feature slips again. Opening the repository starts to feel heavy. Then a person stops showing up, sometimes without a clean goodbye.

Work built around one person's heroics breaks in exactly this way, and no pep talk fixes it. The work has to change.

So we started Subculture Collective with sustainability as a design constraint. We protect people's ability to step back. Contributions can be small, and absence does not incur a social debt. We share enough context that progress does not depend on one person absorbing every interruption. That is what makes coming back possible.

## Habits we keep

We prefer small changes that can be reversed. Large rewrites put too much knowledge in too few hands. Incremental work is easier to review, easier to learn from, and less intimidating to pick up.

We state the scope plainly. Vague expectations make people guess, and conscientious contributors often fill the gap with extra work nobody asked for.

We try to keep a rejected idea from feeling like a rejected person. That line blurs quickly in creative work. Reviews should give reasons, leave room for disagreement, and let a proposal end without turning its author into the problem.

We are suspicious of urgency as a default tone. A feature can wait. Many bugs can too. Calling every delay a crisis spends attention we may need for the problems that cannot wait.

People also need to question a decision or try an uncertain idea without bracing for humiliation. If criticism brings disproportionate backlash, contributors play it safe or leave, and we lose their stranger, more interesting ideas first.

And we write down why. Direction can drift quietly: contributors wonder whether their work still fits, but nobody has named what changed. We document the reasons behind our choices so people can orient themselves and argue with the direction when needed.

## What we give up

Subculture Collective does not chase maximum output. We accept slower timelines, say no to features we cannot maintain, and plan around the fact that people have lives outside the repository. Human beings are not interchangeable compute units.

In our experience, respecting those limits gives a project a better chance of lasting. When participation feels heavy, we inspect how the work is organized before we question anyone's commitment.

We want contribution to be an invitation with a real option to decline, and we want people to finish a session with some energy left for the rest of life. A project that repeatedly exhausts its contributors is already failing them. We will miss this standard sometimes. It is still the one we use.
