# Media without a studio: small-crew production in the age of assembly lines

Every short video passes through decisions about structure, shots, captions, credits, export, and release. A studio assigns those decisions to departments. A person working alone has the same jobs and less time.

In practice it goes like this. A phone fills up with footage. One clip has the clean audio. Another has the right gesture. The notes are somewhere else, and the edit stalls before anyone sees a rough cut.

Independent media needs production support that does not seize the work. The stages should be visible, the next decision should be clear, and each handoff should preserve authorship.

Two incubating SUBCULT projects approach that need at different scales. Cutroom is a proposed collaborative AI video pipeline built around specialized agents, structured handoffs, and attribution. One Block Cinema is a smartphone filmmaking coach for structured one-to-three-minute shorts. It starts from the device someone already has and a form small enough to finish. Neither is a deployed answer yet. They are attempts to make production easier to inhabit without copying a studio hierarchy.

Media automation usually aims at replacement. It can draft a script, synthesize a voice, or assemble a cut. More output does not settle why this piece should exist, whose view it carries, or who gets credit. Useful automation carries context between stages instead. It can organize assets, flag a continuity problem, or prepare captions for review, while the author still decides what belongs in the piece and when it is done.

That boundary has to stay legible. A generated suggestion needs a clear source. Credits need to survive the export. Review needs to happen before distribution, while a person can still change the work.

Small constraints help too. A block gives the shoot a location. A short runtime limits the footage. Available light removes a setup. Each limit takes a decision away and moves the film closer to a finished cut.

So do the dull parts. Stable filenames keep footage attached to notes. A visible production stage keeps a draft from vanishing between edit and captioning. A plain archive makes later reuse possible.

Platforms offer to absorb much of this. In return they shape the format, tune distribution for engagement, and tie the archive to an account. The convenience can turn into editorial control without anyone announcing it. A workshop keeps the files and decisions with the people making the piece. Tools can assist without becoming the publisher, the rights holder, or the gatekeeper to the audience.

That workshop may be one person, a phone, and a few dependable steps, with software checking the tedious parts. Its job is to help a small crew finish the work without giving away its shape.

The machine can carry the clipboard. The author keeps the final cut.
