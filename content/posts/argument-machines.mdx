# Argument machines: markets, courts, and the usefulness of structured disagreement

Before an autonomous system spends money or changes a shared resource, somebody should be able to stop it. That sounds obvious. In software, the objection often arrives after execution, dressed up as a log entry.

The omission is easy to miss at full speed. A signal comes in, a model interprets it, and an action goes out. The clean path looks efficient because context and doubt have nowhere to enter.

Markets and courts offer useful procedures for this problem. A market lets people act on incompatible beliefs through bids and offers. A court assigns roles, admits evidence, hears objections, and records a decision.

Both give disagreement authority and consequence. That is what makes them argument machines.

Several Subcult projects test this idea in different settings. augr runs market analysis through debate before execution. JuryRigged gives a courtroom simulation fixed phases and jury voting.

Subcorp is still incubating. Its design explores roundtable conversations and autonomous workflows in a self-hosted multi-agent system. It should be read as work in progress, not as proof that every governance problem has been solved.

Adding another agent does little on its own. An objection matters only when the system knows who can raise it, what evidence supports it, and whether it can pause the next action.

The answer depends on the stakes. A trading workflow may need exposure limits before an order. A courtroom simulation needs a coherent route from testimony to verdict. An autonomous office needs a way to catch mission drift.

The review should fit the decision. Some actions need a risk check. Others need a dissenting recommendation or a human approval. Afterward, the system needs enough history to compare the prediction with the result.

Those pauses are part of the work. They keep a confident output from passing as a settled decision, and they leave a record when the minority view turns out to be right.

This is governance inside product design. More power to act calls for clearer routes to object.

> Autonomy without disagreement is just automation with better branding.

Leave the gavels and trading floor where they are. Software needs a decision procedure that takes incompatible interpretations seriously before an irreversible step.

Execution can stay fast where the stakes are low. Where the stakes rise, the argument belongs in the path, with enough memory to show whose judgment held up.
