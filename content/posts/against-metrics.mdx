# Against metrics

A number can answer a useful question. Put the same number at the top of a dashboard and rank people by it, and the question changes.

Follower counts make visibility look like legitimacy. Engagement rates make a reaction look like substance. People go along because ignoring those numbers can cost reach, work, or income. A target does more than describe activity. It gives everyone a reason to shape their work around it. The number is precise, and what it means usually is not.

A community may care about trust, a usable archive, or room to experiment without an audience watching. A spreadsheet can record pieces of that, but it cannot add them up into cultural value. The platform dashboards we know lead with impressions, clicks, retention, and conversion. Those figures record real events, and then the dashboard presents them as the purpose of the work. When a drop in visibility is the only feedback on offer, it is easy to read it as a verdict.

We separate ranking from measurement. A weekly growth figure can show what changed. It cannot say whether the change helped. For that we have to ask whether the archive became easier to use, whether the right people found it, and whether maintaining it asked too much of the community. None of that fits on a leaderboard, and all of it is closer to what we need to know.

Mandatory growth has a cost. In our experience on engagement-driven platforms, heated posts often travel farther than careful ones. Creators learn what gets distributed, and the work shifts in response.

Leaving the dashboard is no answer either. Organizations need feedback, budgets, and signs that a project works. So we show trends without public rankings. We explain an unusual change instead of celebrating every spike, because a rise might be healthy interest, unwanted attention, or a burst of work that exhausts the maintainers, and the chart cannot tell which. We ask what a project is trying to accomplish before choosing what to count.

Two rules come out of this. When a number adds pressure but no understanding, take it out of view. If the team cannot say what decision it informs, stop collecting it.
