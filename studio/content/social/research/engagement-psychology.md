# Engagement psychology for social drafts

Research checked 2026-10-05. This file collects what published research says about why people stop for, read, believe, reply to and share a post. Use it with [platform-playbooks.md](platform-playbooks.md), [growth-strategy.md](growth-strategy.md), [visuals.md](visuals.md) and the compact [DIGEST.md](DIGEST.md). [VOICE.md](../../VOICE.md) still decides how each account sounds, and the [Product Claims Register](../Product%20Claims%20Register.md) still decides what may be claimed.

## Evidence tags

- **platform-documented**: the platform says it in its own documentation, code, blog or an executive's on-record statement.
- **research-backed**: a peer-reviewed study, meta-analysis or large published dataset with a stated method. Most of these studies are about news headlines, ads or customer service, not small creator accounts; the transfer to our posts is an inference.
- **practitioner consensus**: repeated advice from credible practitioners or vendor datasets (Buffer and similar), usually observational.
- **folklore/unverified**: widely repeated, with no traceable source, or contradicted by better evidence.

## 1. What makes people share

**High-arousal emotion predicts sharing more than positive or negative tone alone.** In three months of New York Times articles, content that evoked awe, anger or anxiety was more likely to reach the most-emailed list; sadness (low arousal) predicted less sharing. Positive content was shared more than negative overall. Practical usefulness, interest and surprise were also positively linked to sharing, after controlling for placement. *[research-backed]* ([Berger & Milkman 2012, Journal of Marketing Research](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228137203_What_Makes_Online_Content_Viral))

For us: awe at something working, and anger at a specific injustice, are legitimate. Manufactured outrage and vague excitement are not. Practical value ("here is how to do X without paying Y") is the most usable driver for software posts.

**People share for their own reasons: to look informed, to help, to bond.** Berger's review sorts word of mouth into five functions: impression management, emotion regulation, information acquisition, social bonding and persuasion. Interesting, novel and useful things get shared partly because sharing them makes the sharer look good. *[research-backed]* ([Berger 2014, Journal of Consumer Psychology, review PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/WOM-Review.pdf))

A post is easier to forward when it gives the reader something to say ("this person built a free archive of every Hasan stream") rather than something to promote.

**STEPPS (social currency, triggers, emotion, public, practical value, stories)** is Berger's popular synthesis of this work. Treat it as a checklist, not a law. *[practitioner consensus, built on research]* ([summary of *Contagious*](https://www.supersummary.com/contagious-jonah-berger/summary/))

**Posts attacking a political out-group get about twice the shares of posts about one's own side.** Across 2.7 million posts from news media and US congressional accounts on Facebook and Twitter, out-group language was the strongest predictor of shares and retweets measured. *[research-backed]* ([Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden 2021, PNAS](https://www.jstor.org/stable/27040793)) People also say they do not want platforms to amplify divisive content. *[research-backed]* ([Rathje et al. 2024, Perspectives on Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/17456916231190392))

For Patrick's political posts: criticism of a specific actor with a specific source is fair. Writing for dunk value buys reach and damages trust. It is a known mechanism, so a reviewer should notice when a draft relies on it.

**Negative words raise headline clicks a little.** In 22,743 Upworthy headline A/B tests (370 million impressions), each additional negative word raised click-through by about 2.3% for an average-length headline, and positive words lowered it. *[research-backed]* ([Robertson et al. 2023, Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4)) The effect is small per word and measured on clicks, not trust or follows.

## 2. Curiosity and the first line

**Curiosity comes from noticing a small, specific gap in what you know.** Loewenstein's information-gap theory holds that curiosity rises when attention falls on a gap between what one knows and wants to know; a small, well-defined gap produces more curiosity than total ignorance. *[research-backed]* ([Loewenstein 1994, Psychological Bulletin, summarized in this review](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/jd-09-2023-0173/full/pdf?title=curiosity-and-information-seeking-behaviour-a-review-of-psychological-research-and-a-comparison-with-the-information-science-literature))

**Too vague and too specific both lose; a middle level of concreteness wins clicks.** A meta-analysis of 8,977 Upworthy headline experiments found an inverted U: when the baseline headline is vague, more concreteness raises click-through; when it is already concrete, more concreteness lowers it. About half the headlines were "too concrete" for maximum clicks, and only 8.7% were too vague. *[research-backed]* ([Aubin Le Quéré & Matias 2025, Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11704130/))

This was measured on headlines that had to win a click. Most of our posts are read in the feed, where the post itself is the payoff. There the first line should open a gap that the rest of the post closes ("Twitch deletes most clips after a while. clpr keeps the ones chat cared about." then how). A link post can hold back slightly more, as long as the destination pays off.

**Curiosity bait that does not pay off costs credibility.** In an experiment on news articles, clickbait headlines lowered readers' perceptions of credibility and quality, and writing proficiency mattered more than whether the story was aggregated. *[research-backed]* ([Molyneux & Coddington, Journalism Practice, 2019 online](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333822648_Aggregation_Clickbait_and_Their_Effect_on_Perceptions_of_Journalistic_Credibility_and_Quality)) Forward-reference headlines ("This is what happened next") grab involuntary attention, which is why they are overused. *[research-backed]* ([ERP study on forward reference](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366652381_Attention_Grabbing_through_Forward_Reference_An_ERP_Study_on_Clickbait_and_Top_News_Stories)) Meta classifies goading people to react, comment, share, tag or vote as engagement bait and demotes it. *[platform-documented]* ([Meta, December 2017](https://about.fb.com/news/2017/12/news-feed-fyi-fighting-engagement-bait-on-facebook/))

**Readers see the first two words of a line, or about 11 characters.** NN/g's eyetracking work found users scanning lists and headlines often read only the first two words; for 35% of tested links, users shown only the first 11 characters could not tell where the link led. Front-load the words that carry meaning. *[research-backed, usability research]* ([NN/g, First 2 Words](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/))

## 3. Specific beats general

**Concrete language makes the writer seem to have listened and makes ideas stick.** In recorded customer-service calls, agents who used concrete words ("that gray T-shirt") produced higher satisfaction, mediated by customers' sense of being heard. *[research-backed]* ([Packard & Berger 2021, Journal of Consumer Research](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344934214_How_Concrete_Language_Shapes_Customer_Satisfaction)) The Heaths' *Made to Stick* argues that simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional stories (SUCCESs) are remembered and retold. *[practitioner consensus, synthesizing research]* ([book summary](https://growthsummary.com/book-summary/made-to-stick/))

Specific means names, numbers we can verify, a real object on screen, a real place, a real time. "Rekolekt keeps a recording archive on hardware you own" beats "own your media". Numbers must come from the claims register.

**Easy-to-read statements are judged more likely true.** Statements printed in high-contrast colors were judged true more often than the same statements in low-contrast colors. *[research-backed]* ([Reber & Schwarz 1999, Consciousness and Cognition](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effects-of-Perceptual-Fluency-on-Judgments-of-Truth-Reber-Schwarz/5a14c99cae5603943848d43273242a4c06e9e72c)) Alter and Oppenheimer review how fluency (ease of processing, from contrast to plain wording) shapes judgments of truth, liking and confidence. *[research-backed]* ([Alter & Oppenheimer 2009, Personality and Social Psychology Review](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~aalter/tribes.pdf))

This works in both directions. Plain wording helps a true claim land. It also makes a false one land, which is why the claims register matters more, not less, when copy is fluent.

**Plain language helps experts too.** NN/g's studies with domain experts found they also wanted succinct, scannable text. *[research-backed, usability research]* ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/)) People scan before they read: in Nielsen's 1997 study, 79% of test users scanned new pages, and concise, scannable and objective rewrites improved measured usability. *[research-backed, old]* ([NN/g, How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)) The F-shaped scanning pattern still appeared on desktop and mobile in 2017. *[research-backed]* ([NN/g, Text Scanning Patterns](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)) Short, simple text is understood as well on phones as on computers; difficult text is slower on phones. *[research-backed]* ([NN/g, Reading Content on Mobile Devices](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/))

## 4. Story, effort and process

**Stories reduce counter-arguing.** Readers who are absorbed in a narrative ("transported") hold more story-consistent beliefs and argue back less. *[research-backed]* ([Green & Brock 2000, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-role-of-transportation-in-the-persuasiveness-of-public-narrat/)) A meta-analysis of consumer narrative transportation found effects on attitudes and intentions, with identifiable characters and plausible plots among the antecedents. *[research-backed]* ([van Laer et al. 2014, Journal of Consumer Research](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2033192))

A 280-character post is too short for transportation in the full sense, but it can carry the bones: a person, a problem, what changed. VOICE.md already asks for this in update posts.

**Seeing the work raises its perceived value.** Buell and Norton found people valued a service more when they could see it working, even when they waited longer, partly through a sense of reciprocity for the effort. *[research-backed]* ([Buell & Norton 2011, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376)) Devlogs and build-in-public posts rely on this.

**Being told something took effort raises quality ratings, but this replicates unevenly.** Kruger et al. found poems and paintings were rated better when people were told they took longer to make. *[research-backed]* ([Kruger et al. 2004, effort heuristic](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222427507_The_effort_heuristic)) A preregistered replication with 1,405 participants got mixed results. *[research-backed]* ([Ziano et al. 2023, Collabra](https://mgto.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Ziano-etal-2023-Collabra-Effort-heuristic-Kruger-etal-2004-replication-extension-print.pdf)) Show the work itself; do not lean on "this took 200 hours" as proof of quality.

**People value what they help build, if it gets finished.** In the IKEA-effect studies, people valued their own assembled furniture, origami and Lego above equivalent items, and the effect disappeared when the item was destroyed or left incomplete. *[research-backed]* ([Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012, Journal of Consumer Psychology](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002)) Inviting a bug report, a feature vote or a test run gives people a stake. Then close the loop and tell them what happened to their contribution.

**Admitting a small flaw may help competent people and hurt others.** Aronson's 1966 experiment found a highly competent contestant who spilled coffee was liked more, while an average contestant who did the same was liked less. *[research-backed, old and small]* ([Aronson, Willerman & Floyd 1966, Psychonomic Science](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03342263.pdf)) A 1970 follow-up found the blunder helped only with observers of average self-esteem, as summarized in [the Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect). We did not find a large modern replication during this pass. Honest maturity notes are justified by accuracy, not by this effect.

## 5. Intimacy and parasocial ties

**Followers form one-sided bonds with creators, and creators deliberately tend them.** Patreon artists frame payments as social support between close parties and reward patrons with self-disclosure and interaction. *[research-backed, qualitative]* ([Hair 2021, Sociological Spectrum, "Friends, not ATMs"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348821620_Friends_not_ATMs_parasocial_relational_work_and_the_construction_of_intimacy_by_artists_on_Patreon)) Patreon creators perform relational labor across platforms. *[research-backed, qualitative]* ([Bonifacio, Hair & Wohn, New Media & Society](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/14614448211027961)) Streamers report that audience members can read a creator's sharing as an invitation, and that relationships do not look the same from both sides. *[research-backed, qualitative]* ([Mediekultur study on streamers and parasocial relationships](https://www.mediekultur.dk/article/download/136534/199631/347611))

For Patrick's account: first-person detail about the work (a tool, a decision, a mistake) builds the bond without oversharing. The SUBCULT "we" account is less personal by design. Neither should imply a closeness or a reply guarantee that the collective cannot keep.

**A direct, personal ask works better than a broadcast.** In two Twitter field experiments with an advocacy group (N = 6,687 and 8,507), followers who got a direct message signed a petition 3.6 and 4.4 percentage points more often than those who only saw the public tweet. *[research-backed]* ([Coppock, Guess & Ternovski 2016, Political Behavior](https://alexandercoppock.com/coppock_guess_ternovski_2016.html)) For the drafting system: broadcast posts do not substitute for asking specific people, by hand, for specific help.

## 6. AI-sounding copy

**Emotional messages lose authenticity when readers think AI wrote them.** In seven preregistered experiments, consumers who believed an emotional marketing message was AI-written reported lower authenticity and more moral disgust, and lower word of mouth and loyalty. The effect shrank for factual messages and when AI only edited a human draft. *[research-backed]* ([Kirk & Givi 2025, Journal of Business Research](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296324004880))

**Suspicion of AI hurts most when readers can compare.** When people saw a mix of profiles, they distrusted the ones they believed AI had written; with all-AI or all-human sets they did not. *[research-backed]* ([Jakesch et al. 2019, CHI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/epdf/10.1145/3290605.3300469)) A feed is a mixed set.

**LLM style has detectable fingerprints.** Since 2023, biomedical abstracts show sharp excess use of style words such as "delves", "underscores", "showcases", "pivotal", "intricate" and "realm"; the authors estimate at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were LLM-processed. *[research-backed]* ([Kobak et al., Science Advances; arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v3)) Repeated sentence shapes (VOICE.md's list) are a stronger tell than single words. Treat these as review clues; banning one word just moves the tell elsewhere.

## 7. Myths and weak numbers

- **"Human attention span is eight seconds, shorter than a goldfish."** The 2015 Microsoft Canada report took the figures from Statistic Brain, which could not produce a source. *[folklore/unverified]* ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91023619/8-second-attention-span-is-bs-this-is-why), [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/t-humans-eight-second-attention-span-goldfish-myth/))
- **"Asking for retweets increases retweets 311%."** This comes from a 2013 Twitter blog post about Promoted Tweets. It is old, about ads, and asking for shares is now classed as bait on Meta. *[platform data, dated 2013]* ([X blog, 2013](https://blog.x.com/en_us/a/2013/tweet-tips-most-effective-calls-to-action-on-twitter))
- **"Effort makes work look better, so say how long it took."** The effort heuristic replicates unevenly (above).
- **"Negativity always wins."** The headline effect is per-word and small, and measured on clicks; arousal, usefulness and awe also predict sharing.
- **"Clickbait works."** It wins some clicks and loses credibility. The concreteness data show vagueness is not the click-maximizing strategy either.

## What this means for drafts

1. Lead with the most concrete, meaningful words; make the first line open a small gap the post closes.
2. Prefer practical value and real moments of awe or anger with a source over generic excitement.
3. Give the reader something they would be glad to forward.
4. Show the work and the person behind it; report maturity honestly.
5. Ask specific people for specific help directly; do not bait the feed.
6. Write plainly, vary the shape, and check the result against VOICE.md's list of repeated shapes.
