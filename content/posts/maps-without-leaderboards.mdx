# Maps without leaderboards: making communities legible without ranking them

A newcomer enters through one group chat, learns the scene through another, and stays because one person keeps making introductions. A message count records almost none of that.

Many communities rely on translators, conflict de-escalators, hosts, archivists, and people who notice when someone drifts away. Their work often happens between the events a dashboard can count. Messages, reactions, followers, and retention answer narrow questions, and then they get used as if they were the community itself.

A network map offers another view. It can reveal clusters that rarely talk, a newcomer stuck at the edge, or one participant carrying every connection between two groups. clustr, our incubating Reddit graph project, explores that view by mapping community relationships and user interactions. It does not capture the full texture of those relationships, and no graph should claim to.

Even a partial map can help. It can also expose people to scrutiny they never agreed to.

Centrality is the easiest part to misuse. Put it in a ranked table and an observation about structure becomes a status prize. People may optimize for the score, while moderators or owners gain a convenient tool for control. The useful reading is practical. If one person connects two groups, too much depends on them, so protect their time and help more people cross between the groups. Do not turn the connection into an award.

When that person leaves, nothing dramatic may happen. Invitations slow down. References stop traveling. Each group becomes less aware of the other. A map sampled over time might catch the drift before a crisis makes it obvious.

## Before collecting anything

Mapmakers need restraint. A community may need private views, coarse aggregation, short retention, or no graph at all. Some relationships are safer as stories held by people who earned the trust to hear them.

Consent cannot be inferred from public activity. A visible comment does not grant permission to profile its author, score their influence, or preserve their associations forever. Before collecting data, the mapmaker should be able to say:

- who can see the result,
- how long the source data stays,
- what harm follows from a leak.

Curiosity is a poor threat model.

Within those limits a map can support maintenance. It can show where newcomers lose contact, which group has no route to shared knowledge, whether moderation labor sits with two people, and whether a local conflict has begun to travel. None of those questions needs a winner. They need enough context for a community to decide where care and capacity are thin.

Leaderboards make hierarchy feel objective. A community map should resist that at every layer, including the data it keeps and the views it refuses to publish. If a map cannot protect the people it describes, do not draw it.
