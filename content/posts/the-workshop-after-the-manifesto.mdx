# The workshop after the manifesto: building a collective without becoming a platform

Six months after a manifesto goes online, its politics show up in duller questions: who can find an abandoned draft, whether a contributor can move their work elsewhere, whether publishing stops when one service changes its terms.

A declaration sets direction. The maintenance record shows whether the collective kept moving after the first rush wore off. The failures are usually mundane. Notes scatter, repositories lose context, and half-built projects become rumors. People spend their limited attention reconstructing decisions that should have been recorded.

For SUBCULT, the workshop currently includes a locally edited project catalog, markdown essays, and source synced through a self-hosted Git service. Those are the parts you can inspect today. A grander inventory would overstate what exists.

The practical question is whether the work can continue without accepting a platform's defaults. Source should stay readable. Content should use formats that move. An outside service can be useful without becoming the only door to the archive. We welcome technology when the collective can inspect it, replace it, and recover from its failure. What we are against is capture.

Small maintenance choices carry the politics. The catalog distinguishes active projects from incubating ones, so unfinished work does not have to pretend it has shipped. The zine keeps an argument around past the life of a feed post.

The workshop also lowers the cost of returning. A clear repository tells the next contributor what happened. A plain archive lets someone recover a piece years later without reconstructing a vanished toolchain. Internal process shapes public work in the same way. Hidden decisions and unclear ownership produce confusion in the artifact, and contributors who can see and move the work have a better chance of keeping control of it.

Capture also happens at our own scale. A collective can build its own mandatory dashboard, treat catalog traffic as a verdict, or force every project through one workflow. Self-hosting alone prevents none of that. So good workshop rules leave room to exit:

- Components should be replaceable.
- Exports should open in common tools.
- Archives should make sense without the original service.
- A dead project should be easy to mark, preserve, or remove.

This discipline can feel inefficient beside a platform that offers one account and an instant audience. That convenience costs the collective control over the format, the relationship with readers, and the conditions of departure. We would rather build tools culture can leave without dying.

Work should continue after any one tool is retired. A project can be archived and understood elsewhere. A contributor can leave without erasing their part of the record.

Maintenance rarely looks like the manifesto. It looks like an export that still opens, a repository with enough context, and no locked door between people and their work.
