# Against metrics

A number can answer a useful question. Put the same number at the top of a dashboard, rank people by it, and the question changes.

Follower counts make visibility look like legitimacy. Engagement rates make a reaction look like substance. People respond to those incentives because ignoring them can cost reach, work, or income.

This is where measurement becomes dangerous. A target does more than describe activity. It gives participants a reason to shape their work around the number.

The number is precise. Its meaning usually is not.

---

A community may care about trust, a usable archive, or room to experiment without an audience watching. A spreadsheet can record pieces of that work, but it cannot sum them into cultural value.

The platform dashboards we know lead with impressions, clicks, retention, and conversion. Those figures record real events. Trouble starts when the dashboard treats them as the purpose of the work.

It is easy to read a drop in visibility as a verdict, especially when that is the only feedback on offer.

> The metric becomes the narrative.

At Subculture Collective, we separate ranking from useful measurement.

A weekly growth figure can show what changed. It cannot decide whether that change helped.

The answer depends on the work. Did the archive become easier to use? Did the right people find it? Did maintaining it ask too much of the community?

Those questions are less tidy than a leaderboard. They are also closer to what we need to know.

---

Mandatory growth has a cost. In our experience on engagement-driven platforms, heated posts often travel farther than careful ones. Creators learn what gets distributed, and the work shifts in response.

Leaving the dashboard is no answer. Organizations need feedback, budgets, and signs that a project works. We want feedback that helps people make decisions without turning the score into an identity.

For us, that means showing trends without public rankings. It means explaining an unusual change instead of celebrating every spike. It means asking what a project is trying to accomplish before choosing what to count.

Numbers belong in context. A rise might reflect healthy interest. It might also come from unwanted attention or a burst of work that exhausts the maintainers. The chart cannot settle that question.

When a number adds pressure but no understanding, remove it from view. If the team cannot say what decision it informs, stop collecting it.

Not everything that can be counted should count.
